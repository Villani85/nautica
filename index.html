04 — How it is made

The cut is not a picture.
It is a camera decision.

The camera never pitches. It looks dead level at the horizon, and that single constraint is what puts the waterline at exactly half the screen, whatever the proportions of the window. The page background can therefore split at 50% with a hard stop and meet the real-time drawing without anything being synchronised.

We had this wrong in writing for weeks, and the correction is worth more than the original claim. Everywhere in our own notes it said the camera sits at height zero, as though the height were the cause. It is not. Point a camera level and the plane of the water projects onto the centre line of the frame from any height — measured: at 1.28 units up, the horizon lands 0.019 px off centre on a 900 px canvas. Drop the camera back to zero and tilt it slightly, and the line leaves immediately.

The half-truth cost us a night. Believing the height was the invariant, we never tried raising the camera; believing height alone was sufficient, we then raised it and found the second half of the rule the hard way. The geometry survives any height. The tone does not: lift the camera and you start looking down onto a lit sea surface, the lower half stops reading as deep water, and the split dies anyway — for a completely different reason.

No code keeps the two halves aligned, because none is needed. That is the difference between an effect and a geometry.

The hull is a loft between stations

Nine sections along the length, interpolated. The hull, the deck and the section cap are curves described in code. The mechanism and the two upper decks are modelled in Blender by the scripts in this repository and shipped as glTF — 280 KB, meshopt-compressed. Still no third-party model, and still nothing downloaded that we did not author; but they are files now, not curves, and the page said zero bytes for one night longer than it was true. And the section cap at any station is the same function that generates the surface — not a second implementation that would silently drift apart. A test proves the two coincide to within 7.2 × 10⁻⁸, the precision of a 32-bit float.

The reduction is measured, not declared

Two simulations run in parallel, identical except that one has no fin authority. The number on screen is one minus the ratio of the two RMS roll amplitudes, taken at steady state and averaged over five independent draws of the sea. It changes with the sea and with the speed, because in reality it does: fins produce lift only when the ship is making way, so at anchor the reduction is zero, and in heavy weather the fins stall and their effectiveness collapses.

It reads RMS rather than peak, and it is worth saying why. The forcing is a sum of three harmonics whose periods do not divide into one another, so they never come back into phase: the largest roll inside any finite window depends on where in that beat you happened to look, and converges on nothing. We measured it — the peak ratio moved by 5.6 points between page loads. The RMS ratio moves by 0.2. RMS is also the quantity the industry quotes, which we found out after choosing it for the other reason.

The live readings above — roll, and peak over ten seconds — stay instantaneous. Being tied to the frame you are looking at is the whole point of a reading. A ratio is not a reading, and needs steady state.

The energy model is declared illustrative

The two bars show an index from 0 to 100, not kilowatts. The multipliers are ours, chosen so the reading is legible, and putting a physical unit on them would be a lie told well.

05 — With your product

This model is generic.
Yours is not.

With your CAD, your data and your product, the same interaction becomes a commercial demonstration: on your site, in the dealer presentation, on the monitor at the trade fair, on the agent’s tablet.

What follows is deliberately specific. A page that says “send us your files” is asking you to guess; these are the files, the numbers and the decisions that actually determine whether this can be built for your product, and how long it takes.

The CAD

STEP is the one that works. AP214 or AP242, solids rather than surfaces. IGES and Parasolid are fine. A native file — SolidWorks, CATIA, NX, Inventor, Creo — is also fine, but it gets converted before anything else happens, and conversion loses the feature tree: what arrives is geometry, not parameters. If the part is configurable and the configuration matters, that has to be described separately.

Simplified is better than complete. What this needs is the silhouette, the moving parts and their axes — not internal fasteners, threads or supplier sub-assemblies. A model stripped down to those is faster to work with and carries less of your intellectual property out of your building.

The numbers that make it move

The physics here is driven by seven quantities. Without them the motion is decoration:

  • fin area and maximum angle — they set the righting moment, and the moment is the whole argument
  • reduction ratio and rated torque at the output
  • maximum angular rate of the fin — a stabiliser that cannot keep up with the wave does not stabilise
  • the vessel it is fitted to: displacement, beam, metacentric height and natural roll period
  • service speed range — fin authority goes with the square of it, and that is the most counter-intuitive thing a buyer learns here
  • the sea states you are willing to be judged on
  • measured reduction, if you have it, and how it was measured

If a number is confidential, it can stay confidential: the demonstration can run on normalised values, exactly as this one does, and say so on the page. What it cannot do is invent them.

Who has to be in the room

One person on your side who can say this may be shown, that may not — and who is authorised to say it. In practice that is rarely marketing alone: markings, declarations of conformity, class approvals and test data all have an owner, and the owner is usually in engineering or quality. Deciding this at the start costs one meeting. Deciding it at the end costs the project.

How long

  • Week 1 — files, numbers, and the shown/not-shown list. Nothing is modelled before this is agreed.
  • Weeks 2–3 — geometry: your part, reduced to what the camera needs, with its real axes and real travel.
  • Weeks 3–4 — physics and gates: your numbers driving the motion, and the tests that fail when they stop being true.
  • Weeks 5–6 — the four surfaces: site, dealer presentation, trade-fair screen, tablet.

Six weeks is the honest figure for a first product, not a sales figure. The part that slips is never the geometry — it is the week when nobody can decide what may be shown.

What we need from you

  • the CAD, simplified, as STEP
  • the seven quantities above, or the ones you can release
  • the technical documentation that already exists
  • one person who decides what may be shown
